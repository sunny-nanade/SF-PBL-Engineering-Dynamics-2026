Title: Making Sure Our Shock Absorber Energy Loss Model Actually Works
1. Why We're Doing This
So, we built a simulation of a shock absorber to study how it loses energy—basically, how it
turns motion into heat. But before we trust the results, we need to check if the physics
inside the simulation makes sense. This report walks through how we validated that.
We did three things. First, we compared our simulation equations to standard textbook
formulas. Second, we checked whether energy is conserved properly (or rather, whether the
energy we lose to damping matches what we expect). Third, we tweaked key parameters like
the damping coefficient and fluid viscosity to see if the simulation reacts in a realistic way.
Shock absorbers are tricky because you've got spring forces, damping forces, friction from
seals, and even gas pressure fighting back. Our simulation tries to capture all that, but
without going overboard on complexity.

4. What Happens When We Change Things?
We wanted to see if the simulation responds the way a real shock absorber would, so we
played with a few knobs.
Damping coefficient (c)
Crank it up, and the oscillation dies down way faster. But you also get bigger forces
transmitted to the car body. Energy loss per cycle goes up linearly—makes sense.
Fluid viscosity
Thicker fluid means more damping and more heat. But if you go too far, you risk cavitation
(air bubbles forming) or overheating. The simulation shows exactly that trend.
Orifice size
Make the little holes in the piston smaller, and the quadratic damping effect kicks in harder
at high speeds. This is how real shock tuners adjust "high-speed compression" damping.
Gas pressure (for gas shocks)
Higher gas pressure helps prevent cavitation and adds a bit of spring force, but it doesn't
change total energy loss over a full cycle by much. Simulation matches reality here too.
Overall, tweaking parameters gives you exactly what you'd expect. So the model passes the
"does it feel right?" test.
5. What Works and What Doesn't
The good news: our simulation does a solid job of capturing how shock absorbers lose
energy. Using both linear and quadratic damping lets us handle low-speed and high-speed
behaviour pretty well.
The spring force, friction, and gas spring terms all work together to make the model behave
like a real suspension component.
But let's be honest—there are some things we left out:
• Temperature changes: real shock fluid gets thinner as it heats up. We don't model
that yet.
• Cavitation: when the fluid vaporizes at high speeds, damping drops off. Not in our
model.
• Seal friction hysteresis: real seals don't behave exactly like simple Coulomb friction.
They have memory effects.
Still, even with these limitations, the model is a solid starting point. Next steps could be
adding thermal effects and validating against dyno test data.
6. Conclusion
We validated our shock absorber simulation by comparing it to textbook equations, checking
energy conservation, and running sensitivity tests. Everything behaves the way physics says
it should.
So yeah—we're confident this model can be used for teaching, suspension design, or just
experimenting with how energy gets turned into heat in mechanical systems. It's not perfect,
but it's physically sound.
